Title and subtitle

Explaining the Lighthouse

A Harbor Light for Bandon

Panel copy131 words

Lighthouses have guided ships for more than 2,000 years, beginning with the Lighthouse of Alexandria in the third century BC. In the United States, the Lighthouse Act of 1789, signed by President George Washington on August 7, created the Lighthouse Establishment. It was the nation's first public works law.

A coast light warns of rocks, shoals, and headlands. A harbor light marks a safe entrance to port. By day, a tower's shape and paint serve as a landmark, called a daymark.

The Coquille River Lighthouse is a harbor light, built to guide ships over the hazardous bar and into the river. Until the north jetty was completed, it stood on a small island. The keepers' house sat 650 feet away on the mainland, across a wooden bridge that regularly washed away.

Title and subtitle

Focusing the Light

The Fourth-Order Fresnel Lens

Panel copy120 words

Early lighthouses burned open fires, candles, or oil lamps. A bare flame sends its light in every direction.

In the 19th century, Augustin Fresnel developed a lens built from hundreds of pieces of cut glass. The glass bends nearly all of a lamp's light into one concentrated beam, visible up to 20 miles or more.

Fresnel lenses are sized by order, with the first order the largest. The Coquille River Lighthouse used a fourth-order lens, similar to the one shown here.

Each light had its own pattern of flashes and eclipses, listed in the government Light List so sailors could tell one light from another. Mariners followed the long glow of the Coquille light to the mouth of the river.

Text box: What Happened to the Lens?49 words

In 1939, the U.S. Coast Guard took over the nation's navigational aids and placed an automated beacon on the south jetty. The keepers' home and outbuildings were torn down, and the lens was removed. Some say it lies in the ocean near the jetties. No one knows for sure.

Title and subtitle

Keeping the Light

Subtitle to be set

Body copy not drafted yet. What this panel covers:

The core duties of lighthouse keepers, including salary, and the Coquille keepers (Langlois, Bretherton, or all).

Suggest wording for the title or subtitle, or what this panel should say.

Background for reference

Working content (Foundation file, as provided)

650 feet away from the light was the keepers residence which was a duplex. Each side had three bedrooms, a kitchen, dinning room, sitting room, and a 15,000-gallon brick water cistern.

A lighthouse keeper was a dedicated custodian responsible for maintaining and operating a lighthouse to guide ships safely, typically living in remote locations and working around the clock to ensure the light stayed lit. Their vital duties included trimming wicks (earning them the nickname "wickies"), cleaning lenses and windows, refueling lamps, winding rotation mechanisms, and keeping detailed logs, especially during stormy weather.

Light Maintenance: Keepers meticulously maintained the light from sunset to sunrise, and sometimes 24 hours a day during storms. Before electricity, this required tending to oil lamps or specialized lanterns.

Upkeep of the Tower: Beyond the lamp, keepers were responsible for cleaning, polishing brass, and painting the tower and surrounding buildings, a role that involved131 regular tasks.

Safety and Inspection: They were required to monitor maritime traffic, keep watch for shipwrecks, and document weather changes, often reporting them officially.

Isolation and Duty: Lighthouse keeping was a lonely profession, often requiring months-long stays in remote, hazardous locations, frequently with little outside contact.

Roles and Rank: Stations often had a Principal Keeper and Assistant Keepers who took turns on watch to ensure the light remained

Legacy of the Keeper: Lighthouse keepers were often seen as heroes for their dedication and bravery in dangerous, isolated conditions, ensuring maritime safety through personal sacrifice. Their work was essential for navigating dangerous coastlines before modern GPS technology.

How is Relief Called: Friction box w extension bells are used between tower & dwellings.

Keepers (Foundation file, as provided)

Oscar Langlois grew up at Cape Blanco lighthouse and became the assistant lighthouse keeper at Cape Argo under headkeeper Lars Fredrik Amundsen. Later he transfered to Coquille lighthouse as an assistant to Captain Wiren. After Wiren retirement he became headkeeper until his retirement.

Oscar Langlois became an assistant keeper of the lighthouse in 1910 and was promoted to head keeper in 1921. He was born at Cape Blanco Lighthouse, where his father served as principal keeper for thirty-five years. Choosing the same career for himself, Oscar accepted his first assignment in 1905 to Cape Arago Lighthouse, where met his wife, Marie Amundsen, daughter of the principal keeper.

During Langlois' lengthy service at Coquille River Lighthouse, a forest fire swept into Bandon in September 1936 and consumed all but sixteen of the towns' 500 buildings. The lighthouse, separated from the fire by a water barrier, was not damaged, however, ash and soot found its way into the lighthouse requiring extra work of the keepers. The sick and invalid from the hospital at Bandon were transported across the river by the lighthouse tender Rose and sheltered at the lighthouse. Keeper Langlois and Charles Walters, his assistant, were commended by the Lighthouse Service for helping refugees during the fire.

Oscar Wiren and Oscar Langlois spent much of their lives tending to the lighthouse here either as assistant or head keepers. Wiren was assistant from 1903-1906 and head from 1906-1921 while Langlois assisted from 1910-1921 and was head from 1921-1939; his father was long-time keeper at nearby Cape Blanco.

The first woman lighthouse keeper in Oregon, Mabel Hatch Bretherton, got her first lighthouse job after her husband, Bernard Bretherton, died in 1903. They married in 1892 in Sitka, Alaska, and had three children. Widowed with three children under 10, Mabel became the assistant lighthouse keeper at Cape Blanco in Port Orford. She then moved to North Head Lighthouse in 1905.

Bernard: thirty years of various jobs including curator of the Oregon Alpine Club and a "collector and preparator of zoological specimens" for museums and taxidermists. US citizenship on January 24, 1891, he went to Alaska to collect specimens of the flora, fauna, and fishes for the British Museum, the Smithsonian Institution (sic), and the US Department of Agriculture (most likely their Division of Ornithology and Mammalogy). He was in Alaska for about three years. He married Mabel Edna Hatch in Sitka, Alaska, on April 30, 1892. Two daughters and a son were born to this marriage. Bernard returned to Oregon with his family in 1894. During the summer that year he led an expedition into the Olympic Mountains to collect mammals and map their geographic distribution for the US Department of Agriculture. Later he gathered specimens of birds in Oregon for the State Agricultural College. He resided at Newport, Oregon, in the mid-1890s, and during this period was commissioned by the British Museum to collect the fishes of Oregon and investigate their habits. This project lasted approximately two years. About the turn of the century, he served as assistant keeper at the Yaquina Bay Lighthouse at Newport, then as keeper at the Coquille River Lighthouse at Bandon, Oregon. After succumbing to the ravages of tuberculosis he died on February 10, 1903 at Coquille River Lighthouse, Bandon, Oregon. He is buried in the parish cemetery at Bandon.

The one idea The lighthouse is an engineered station, built on a leveled river-mouth rock and equipped to guide mariners by light and by sound.

Title and subtitle

Building the Lighthouse

An Exercise in Coastal Engineering

Panel copy, left block69 words

The Coquille River Lighthouse sits atop a large rock known to Indigenous people as Grandfather Rock, later known as Rackliff Rock, named after the captain of a vessel that ran aground at the mouth of the Coquille River. The rock was leveled and prepared as the lighthouse's base, which is situated nearly at sea level at 43° 7′ 26″ N, 124° 25′ 27″ W across the river from Bandon.

Panel copy, right block87 words

A third-class Daboll trumpet foghorn sounded every half minute during heavy fog, backed by a 10-foot cistern, boilers, air compressors, and a pressure tank housed below the tower.

The lighthouse's fourth-order Fresnel lens marked the river's entrance for mariners with a fix light visible seaward for nearly 13 miles. This signal would flash for 28 seconds before a two-second eclipse.

A circular stairway connected the tower to the fog signal room. Leaving that room you cross over to the oil house and mainland on the elevated walkway.

The one idea River commerce built Bandon, and the danger of the bar made a lighthouse the next step.

Title and subtitle

Guiding Bandon's Growth

A Dangerous Price for Progress

Panel copy134 words

By the late 1800s, Bandon had become a major shipping hub between San Francisco and Portland. Euro-American settlers, drawn first by the gold rush, recognized the region's economic potential, and river traffic grew to support timber, fishing, and riverboat travel.

Hazardous fog and storms combined with a treacherous river bar constantly threatened mariners. With the south jetty under construction, a lighthouse at the Coquille River entrance was the next step for safer navigation. In 1890, the Lighthouse Board requested congressional funding; Congress appropriated $50,000 on March 3, 1891, though only $35,000 was needed.

Four years of land purchases, planning, and crew assembly followed. Construction began in April 1895 and finished October 7, 1895. The lighthouse entered service February 29, 1896, following installation of the lighting apparatus and fog signal. It snowed the next day.

Pull quote67 words
“A light of the fourth order with a fog-signal, at this point, would enable vessels bound into the river to hold on close to the bar during the night so that they would be in a position to cross at the next high water. The light would also serve as a coast light and would be of much service to vessels bound up and down the river.”1890 Lighthouse Board request to Congress

Background for reference

Working content (Foundation file, as provided)

Despite the lighthouse standing guard, there were numerous Notable Shipwrecks as a result of this narrow entrance, rough sand bar and unpredictable weather.

The U.S. Lifesaving Service (USLSS) was responsible for rescuing victims of wrecks. They kept watch at all hours and sometimes lost their lives attempting a rescue. Their unofficial motto was "We have to go out; we don't have to return". On January 28, 1915 the Revenue Cutter Service merged with the U.S. Life-Saving Service to form the Coast Guard. In 1946 Congress permanently transferred the Bureau of Marine Inspection and Navigation to the Coast Guard, thereby placing merchant marine licensing and merchant vessel safety under their purview. Their legacy of honor and fearlessness survive to this day. The Lighthouse Service merged with the Coast Guard in 1939; the same year the Coquille Lighthouse was decommissioned.
